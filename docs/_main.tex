\chapter{Complex Numbers}\label{complex-numbers}

A complex number is defined as \(z = x + iy\), where \(x, y \in \mathbb{R}\). Here, \(x\) represents the \textbf{real part} (\(\operatorname{Re}z\)) and \(y\) represents the \textbf{imaginary part} (\(\operatorname{Im}z\)).

The set of all such numbers, \(\mathbb{C}\), constitutes the complex plane. Since \(\mathbb{R} \subset \mathbb{C}\), real numbers are identified as complex numbers where \(\operatorname{Im}z = 0\).

There exists a bijective correspondence between \(\mathbb{C}\) and the Euclidean plane \(\mathbb{R}^2\), expressed as:

\[z = x + iy \longleftrightarrow (x, y)\]

\pandocbounded{\includegraphics[keepaspectratio]{_main_files/_main_files/figure-latex/unnamed-chunk-1-1.pdf}}
In this geometric representation:

\begin{itemize}
\tightlist
\item
  The \textbf{real axis} corresponds to the \(x\)-axis.
\item
  The \textbf{imaginary axis} (\(i\mathbb{R}\)), containing purely imaginary numbers of the form \(iy\), corresponds to the \(y\)-axis.
\end{itemize}

\subsection{Basic Definitions and Algebra}\label{basic-definitions-and-algebra}

\begin{itemize}
\tightlist
\item
  A complex number has the form \(z = a + ib\) where \(a, b\) are real numbers
\item
  \(a\) is the \textbf{real part}: \(\text{Re}(z) = a\)
\item
  \(b\) is the \textbf{imaginary part}: \(\text{Im}(z) = b\)
\item
  A number is \textbf{purely imaginary} if \(a = 0\)
\item
  A number is \textbf{real} if \(b = 0\)
\item
  Zero is the only number that is both real and purely imaginary
\end{itemize}

  \end{itemize}
\end{enumerate}

\subsection{Fundamental Theorem of Algebra}\label{fundamental-theorem-of-algebra}

A complex polynomial of degree \(n \ge 0\) is a function of the form

\[
p(z) = a_n z^n + a_{n-1} z^{\,n-1} + \cdots + a_1 z + a_0,
\qquad z \in \mathbb{C},
\]

where \(a_0, a_1, \ldots, a_n\) are complex numbers and \(a_n \neq 0\).

A fundamental property of the complex numbers is that every polynomial
with complex coefficients can be factored completely into linear factors.

\begin{theorem}[Fundamental Theorem of Algebra]
\protect\hypertarget{thm:unnamed-chunk-5}{}\label{thm:unnamed-chunk-5}Every complex polynomial \(p(z)\) of degree \(n \ge 1\) admits a factorization

\[
p(z) = c\,(z - \zeta_1)^{m_1} (z - \zeta_2)^{m_2} \cdots (z - \zeta_k)^{m_k},
\]

where the numbers \(\zeta_1, \ldots, \zeta_k\) are distinct complex roots of \(p\),
each multiplicity satisfies \(m_j \ge 1\), and \(m_1 + m_2 + \cdots + m_k = n.\)
This factorization is \textbf{unique}, up to a permutation of the factors.
\end{theorem}

\begin{proof}
\emph{Omitted.} Multiple complete and rigorous proofs are available at
\href{https://proofwiki.org/wiki/Fundamental_Theorem_of_Algebra}{ProofWiki}.
\end{proof}

The uniqueness of the factorization is straightforward.
The points \(\zeta_1, \ldots, \zeta_k\) are uniquely determined as the zeros of \(p(z)\),
that is, the solutions of \(p(z)=0\). Each multiplicity \(m_j\) is the unique
integer \(m \ge 1\) for which \(p(z)\) can be written in the form
\((z-\zeta_j)^m q(z)\) with \(q(\zeta_j)\neq 0\).

To prove that such a factorization exists, one uses induction on the degree
\(n\) of the polynomial. The key step is to show that \(p(z)\) has at least one
root \(\zeta_1\). Once a root is found, the polynomial can be factored as
\(p(z) = (z-\zeta_1) q(z)\), where \(q(z)\) has degree \(n-1\). By the induction
hypothesis, \(q(z)\) can be factored into linear factors, which completes the
factorization of \(p(z)\). Thus, the Fundamental Theorem of Algebra is
equivalent to the statement that every complex polynomial of degree
\(n \ge 1\) has at least one zero.

\subsection{Polar Form}\label{polar-form}

\begin{itemize}
\tightlist
\item
  A complex number can be written in \textbf{polar form}:

  \begin{itemize}
  \tightlist
  \item
    \(z = r(\cos\theta + i\sin\theta)\) where \(r = |z|\) and \(\theta = \arg(z)\)
  \item
    \(r\) is the modulus: \(r = |z| = \sqrt{a^2 + b^2}\)
  \item
    \(\theta\) is the \textbf{argument} or \textbf{amplitude}: \(\theta = \arg(z)\)
  \end{itemize}
\item
  Properties of polar form:

  \begin{itemize}
  \tightlist
  \item
    For \(z_1 = r_1(\cos\theta_1 + i\sin\theta_1)\) and \(z_2 = r_2(\cos\theta_2 + i\sin\theta_2)\):
  \item
    \(z_1z_2 = r_1r_2[\cos(\theta_1 + \theta_2) + i\sin(\theta_1 + \theta_2)]\)
  \item
    \(\arg(z_1z_2) = \arg(z_1) + \arg(z_2)\)
  \item
    \(\arg\left(\frac{z_1}{z_2}\right) = \arg(z_1) - \arg(z_2)\)
  \end{itemize}
\end{itemize}

\subsection{De Moivre's Formula}\label{de-moivres-formula}

\begin{itemize}

  \begin{itemize}
  \tightlist
  \item
    \(z^n = r^n(\cos n\theta + i\sin n\theta)\)
  \end{itemize}
\item
  For \(r = 1\):

  \begin{itemize}
  \tightlist
  \item
    \((\cos\theta + i\sin\theta)^n = \cos n\theta + i\sin n\theta\)
  \end{itemize}
\end{itemize}

\subsection{Roots of Complex Numbers}\label{roots-of-complex-numbers}

\begin{itemize}
\tightlist
\item
  The \(n\)-th roots of \(z = r(\cos\theta + i\sin\theta)\) are:

  \begin{itemize}
  \tightlist
  \item
    \(\sqrt[n]{z} = \sqrt[n]{r}\left[\cos\left(\frac{\theta + 2\pi k}{n}\right) + i\sin\left(\frac{\theta + 2\pi k}{n}\right)\right]\) for \(k = 0, 1, 2, \ldots, n-1\)
  \end{itemize}
\item
  There are \(n\) distinct \(n\)-th roots of any non-zero complex number
\item
  These roots have equal moduli and are equally spaced around a circle
\item
  For \(z = 1\), the roots are called the \textbf{\(n\)-th roots of unity}
\end{itemize}

\subsection{Equations in the Complex Plane}\label{equations-in-the-complex-plane}

\begin{itemize}
\tightlist
\item
  Circle with center \(a\) and radius \(r\): \(|z - a| = r\)

  \begin{itemize}
  \tightlist
  \item
    In algebraic form: \((z - a)(\bar{z} - \bar{a}) = r^2\)
  \end{itemize}
\item

  \begin{itemize}
  \tightlist
  \item

  \item

  \item

  \end{itemize}
\end{itemize}

\subsection{Stereographic Projection and the Riemann Sphere}\label{stereographic-projection-and-the-riemann-sphere}

The extended complex plane is the complex plane together with the
point at infinity. We denote the it by \(C^* = c\cup\{\infty\}\).
- The extended complex plane includes \(\infty\) and can be represented on a unit sphere
- Under stereographic projection, the point \((x_1, x_2, x_3)\) on the sphere corresponds to:
- \(z = \frac{x_1 + ix_2}{1 - x_3}\)

\begin{itemize}
\tightlist
\item
  The north pole \((0,0,1)\) corresponds to \(\infty\)
\item
  Properties:

  \begin{itemize}
  \tightlist
  \item
    Circles on the sphere correspond to circles or lines in the plane
  \item
    The distance formula: \(d(z,z') = \frac{2|z-z'|}{\sqrt{(1+|z|^2)(1+|z'|^2)}}\)
  \item
    The sphere is often called the \textbf{Riemann sphere}
  \end{itemize}
\end{itemize}

\chapter{Riemann Surfaces.}\label{riemann-surfaces.}

\begin{definition}[Riemann surface]
\protect\hypertarget{def:unnamed-chunk-8}{}\label{def:unnamed-chunk-8}

A \textbf{Riemann surface} is a second‑countable, Hausdorff topological space \(X\) equipped with a \textbf{complex atlas} \(\mathcal{A} = \{(U_\alpha, \varphi_\alpha)\}\) satisfying the following properties:

\begin{enumerate}
\def\labelenumi{\arabic{enumi}.}
\item
  Each \(U_\alpha \subseteq X\) is open.
\item
  Each map \(\varphi_\alpha : U_\alpha \to V_\alpha\)
  is a homeomorphism onto an open set \(V_\alpha \subseteq \mathbb{C}\).
\item
  The collection \(\{U_\alpha\}\) covers \(X\).
\item
  For every pair of charts \((U_\alpha, \varphi_\alpha)\) and \((U_\beta, \varphi_\beta)\) with \(U_\alpha \cap U_\beta \neq \varnothing,\)
  the \textbf{transition map}
  \[
  \varphi_\beta \circ \varphi_\alpha^{-1} :
  \varphi_\alpha(U_\alpha \cap U_\beta) \longrightarrow
  \varphi_\beta(U_\alpha \cap U_\beta)
  \]
  is a \textbf{biholomorphism}.
\end{enumerate}

\end{definition}

\textbf{Terminology}:
-``holomorphic functions'' are functions which are complex differentiable on an open set in the complex plane.
-A ``biholomorphic map'' is a holomorphic bijection whose inverse is holomorphic.
\end{remark}

\begin{definition}
\protect\hypertarget{def:unnamed-chunk-10}{}\label{def:unnamed-chunk-10}Let \(R_1\) and \(R_2\) be Riemann surfaces. A map \(f : R_1 \to R_2\)
is said to be \textbf{holomorphic} if for every pair of charts \((U, \varphi) \quad \text{on } R_1, \qquad (V, \psi) \quad \text{on } R_2,\) the map \(\psi \circ f \circ \varphi^{-1}\) is holomorphic on its domain (which may be empty).
\end{definition}

Suppose \((U, \varphi)\), \((U', \varphi')\) are charts on \(R_1\), and \((V, \psi)\), \((V', \psi')\) are charts on \(R_2\). On the overlap where all maps are defined, we have:

\begin{itemize}
\tightlist

\item

\end{itemize}

Thus the notion of holomorphicity does \textbf{not} depend on the choice of charts.

Now take any other charts \((U_1,\phi_1)\) at \(p\) and \((V_1,\psi_1)\) at \(f(p)\), with \(f(U_1) \subset V_1\). On overlaps, the transition maps \(\phi \circ \phi_1^{-1} \quad \text{and} \quad \psi_1 \circ \psi^{-1}\) are biholomorphic (holomorphic with holomorphic inverses), because they are chart transition maps on a Riemann surface.

On the intersection where everything is defined, we can write \(\psi_1 \circ f \circ \phi_1^{-1}
= (\psi_1 \circ \psi^{-1}) \circ (\psi \circ f \circ \phi^{-1}) \circ (\phi \circ \phi_1^{-1}).\)

\begin{itemize}
\tightlist
\item
  \(\psi \circ f \circ \phi^{-1}\) is holomorphic by assumption.
\item
  \(\phi \circ \phi_1^{-1}\) and \(\psi_1 \circ \psi^{-1}\) are holomorphic (indeed biholomorphic) transition maps.
\end{itemize}

A composition of holomorphic maps is holomorphic, so \(\psi_1 \circ f \circ \phi_1^{-1}\) is holomorphic.

Since this holds for any charts \((U_1,\phi_1)\), \((V_1,\psi_1)\) around \(p\) and \(f(p)\), it follows that \(f\) is holomorphic by the definition of a holomorphic map between Riemann surfaces.

\subsection{Functions defnied on the Riemann Surface.}\label{functions-defnied-on-the-riemann-surface.}

\subsubsection{Exp function}\label{exp-function}

We know that \(\exp z\) is a holomorphic function on \(\mathbb{C}\) which is periodic in the sense that \(\exp(z + 2\pi i n) = \exp(z)\) for all \(n \in \mathbb{Z}.\) Thus this passes to a well-defined function on \(R\). Namely,
\begin{align}
\operatorname{Exp} : R &\longrightarrow \mathbb{C}\\
[z] &\longmapsto \exp(z)
\end{align}

where \([z]\) is the equivalence class of \(z\).

\begin{exercise}
\protect\hypertarget{exr:unnamed-chunk-14}{}\label{exr:unnamed-chunk-14}Verify that this is well-defined.
\end{exercise}

\textbf{Solution}: To see that \(\operatorname{Exp}\) is well-defined, assume \(z \sim w\).
Then there is an integer \(n \in \mathbb{Z}\) such that \(z = w + 2\pi i\, n.\) Hence,

\[
\operatorname{Exp}([z]) = e^{z}
= e^{\,w + 2\pi i n}
= e^{w}\, e^{2\pi i n}
= e^{w}
= \operatorname{Exp}([w]).
\]

Thus the value of \(\operatorname{Exp}([z])\) depends only on the equivalence class \([z]\),
so the function is well-defined.

\medskip

\textbf{Holomorphicity of \(\operatorname{Exp}\).}
To see that \(\operatorname{Exp}\) is holomorphic, observe that for any chart
\((U_{z_0,r}, \phi_{z_0,r})\) we have

\[
\operatorname{Id}\circ\operatorname{Exp} \circ \phi_{z_0,r}^{-1}(z)
= e^{z},
\]

which is holomorphic on \(\mathbb{C}\).\\
Thus \(\operatorname{Exp}\) is holomorphic by definition.

We may similarly define the function
\(\operatorname{Sin}([z]) = \sin z,\)
which is again well-defined and holomorphic.

We then have a well-defined function

\begin{align}
\operatorname{LOG} : R &\to \mathbb{C}, \\
\operatorname{LOG}(r,\theta) &= \log r + i\theta.
\end{align}

For any \(\theta_0\), the function

\[
\operatorname{LOG} \circ \phi_{\theta_0}^{-1}
\]

is a branch of the logarithm on the plane minus the slit

\[
\operatorname{LOG} \circ \phi_{\theta_0}^{-1}(z) = \log z,
\]

where the right-hand side denotes the branch of the logarithm whose domain

\section{The Riemann sphere}\label{the-riemann-sphere}

As a set, the \textbf{Riemann sphere} is \(\widehat{\mathbb{C}} = \mathbb{C} \cup \{\infty\}.\) To make this into a Riemann surface, we must specify a \textbf{topology} and an \textbf{atlas}.

A basis for the topology consists of:

\begin{enumerate}
\def\labelenumi{\arabic{enumi}.}
\item
  Sets of the form \(U \subset \mathbb{C},\)
  where \(U\) is open in \(\mathbb{C}\) and \(\infty \notin U\).
\item
  Sets of the form \(\{\infty\} \cup (\mathbb{C} \setminus K),\) where \(K \subset \mathbb{C}\) is compact.
\end{enumerate}

Equivalently, a set \(V \subset \widehat{\mathbb{C}}\) is open iff:

\begin{itemize}
\item
  If \(p \in \mathbb{C}\), then there exists an open set \(U \subset \mathbb{C}\) with \(p \in U \subset V.\)
\item
  If \(p = \infty\), then there exists a compact \(K \subset \mathbb{C}\) such that \(\{\infty\} \cup (\mathbb{C} \setminus K) \subset V.\)
\end{itemize}

\begin{exercise}
\protect\hypertarget{exr:unnamed-chunk-18}{}\label{exr:unnamed-chunk-18}Show that \(\widehat{\mathbb{C}}\) is compact.
\end{exercise}

We define an atlas consisting of two charts:

\[
U_1 = \widehat{\mathbb{C}} \setminus \{\infty\}, \qquad 
\varphi_1(z) = z.
\]
\[
U_2 = \widehat{\mathbb{C}} \setminus \{0\}, \qquad 
\varphi_2(z) = \frac{1}{z}.
\]

The first chart and its inverse are clearly continuous.

To see that \(\varphi_2\) is continuous, note that it maps \(U_1 \cap U_2 = \widehat{\mathbb{C}} \setminus \{0,\infty\}\) bijectively onto \(\mathbb{C} \setminus \{0\}\).

The overlap maps are:
\[
\varphi_2 \circ \varphi_1^{-1}(z) = \frac{1}{z}, \qquad
\varphi_1 \circ \varphi_2^{-1}(w) = \frac{1}{w}.
\]

These are biholomorphic, since the inverse of \(z \mapsto 1/z\) is again \(z \mapsto 1/z\).

Thus, this defines a valid atlas, and the resulting Riemann surface is the \textbf{Riemann sphere}.

\subsection{Stereographic Projection and the Metric}\label{stereographic-projection-and-the-metric}

We identify the Riemann sphere with the unit sphere \(S^2 = \{(x_1,x_2,x_3) \in \mathbb{R}^3 : x_1^2 + x_2^2 + x_3^2 = 1\}.\) Think of \(\mathbb{C}\) as the plane \((x_1,y_1,0)\). Given \(z = x + iy \in \mathbb{C}\), draw the line from the north pole \((0,0,1)\) to \((x,y,0)\).\\
This intersects the sphere at:

\begin{align*}
x_1 &= \frac{2x}{|z|^2+1}
     = \frac{2x}{x^2 + y^2 + 1},\\
x_2 &= \frac{2y}{|z|^2+1}
     = \frac{2y}{x^2 + y^2 + 1},\\
x_3 &= \frac{|z|^2-1}{|z|^2+1}
     = \frac{x^2 + y^2 - 1}{x^2 + y^2 + 1}.
\end{align*}

Define
\[
\sigma : S^2 \to \widehat{\mathbb{C}}
\]
by
\[
\sigma(x_1,x_2,x_3) =
\begin{cases}
\dfrac{x_1}{1 - x_3} + i\,\dfrac{x_2}{1 - x_3}, & (x_1,x_2,x_3) \neq (0,0,1), \\[1em]
\infty, & (x_1,x_2,x_3) = (0,0,1).
\end{cases}
\]

\[
\sigma^{-1}(z) =
\begin{cases}
\left(
\dfrac{2\Re z}{|z|^2 + 1},
\dfrac{2\Im z}{|z|^2 + 1},
\dfrac{|z|^2 - 1}{|z|^2 + 1}
\right), & z \in \mathbb{C}, \\[1em]
(0,0,1), & z = \infty.
\end{cases}
\]

The spherical (or chordal) metric is:
\[
d_{\widehat{\mathbb{C}}}(z,w)|
= \frac{2|z-w|}{\sqrt{(1+|z|^2)(1+|w|^2)}},
\]
with the natural extension when one point is \(\infty\).
