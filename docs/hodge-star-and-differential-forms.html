3.1 Basic \(k\)-Forms

3.1.1 Definitions and Structure

Definition 3.1 (Basic One-Forms) For each \(i \in \{1,\ldots,n\}\), the \(dx_i\) is the linear map \(dx_i : \mathbb{R}^n \longrightarrow \mathbb{R}\) defined by projecting a vector onto its \(i\)-th coordinate. If \(\mathbf{u} = (u_1,\ldots,u_n) \in \mathbb{R}^n\), then \[ dx_i(u_1,\ldots,u_n) = u_i. \]

3.1.2 Basic \(k\)-Forms

This algebraic understanding of the basic one-forms as linear maps allows us to define a natural generalization. Instead of looking only at linear maps, we now also define \(k\)-forms on \(\mathbb{R}^n\). The definition relies on the determinant.

Definition 3.2 (Basic Two-Form) The basic two-form \(dx_i \wedge dx_j\) takes two vectors \(\mathbf{u}, \mathbf{v} \in \mathbb{R}^n\) and maps them to the following determinant:

\[dx_i \wedge dx_j (\mathbf{u}, \mathbf{v}) = \det \begin{pmatrix} u_i & v_i \\ u_j & v_j \end{pmatrix} = u_i v_j - u_j v_i\]

Definition 3.3 (Basic Three-Forms) The basic three-form \(dx_i \wedge dx_j \wedge dx_k\) takes three vectors \(\mathbf{u}, \mathbf{v}, \mathbf{w} \in \mathbb{R}^n\) and maps them to:

\[dx_i \wedge dx_j \wedge dx_k (\mathbf{u}, \mathbf{v}, \mathbf{w}) = \det \begin{pmatrix} u_i & v_i & w_i \\ u_j & v_j & w_j \\ u_k & v_k & w_k \end{pmatrix}\]

Definition 3.4 (Basic $k$-Forms) For \(1 \le i_1 < i_2 < \dots < i_k \le n\), the basic \(k\)-form \(dx_{i_1} \wedge \dots \wedge dx_{i_k}\) acts on \(k\) vectors \(\mathbf{u}^1, \dots, \mathbf{u}^k\) via:

\[dx_{i_1} \wedge \dots \wedge dx_{i_k}(\mathbf{u}^1, \dots, \mathbf{u}^k) = \det \begin{pmatrix} u_{i_1}^1 & \dots & u_{i_1}^k \\ \vdots & \ddots & \vdots \\ u_{i_k}^1 & \dots & u_{i_k}^k \end{pmatrix}\]

Lemma 3.1 (Antisymmetry of Basic Form) For any vectors \(\mathbf{u}, \mathbf{v}\):

\[\begin{aligned} dx_i \wedge dx_j (\mathbf{u}, \mathbf{v}) &=-(dx_i \wedge dx_j (\mathbf{u}, \mathbf{v}))\\ dx_i \wedge dx_i &= 0\end{aligned}\]

Proof. For any vectors \(\mathbf{u}, \mathbf{v}\):

\[\begin{aligned} dx_i \wedge dx_j (\mathbf{u}, \mathbf{v}) &= \begin{vmatrix} u_i & v_i \\ u_j & v_j \end{vmatrix} = u_i v_j - u_j v_i \\ dx_j \wedge dx_i (\mathbf{u}, \mathbf{v}) &= \begin{vmatrix} u_j & v_j \\ u_i & v_i \end{vmatrix} = u_j v_i - u_i v_j = -(dx_i \wedge dx_j (\mathbf{u}, \mathbf{v})) \end{aligned}\] Thus, \(dx_i \wedge dx_j = - dx_j \wedge dx_i\), which implies: \[dx_i \wedge dx_i = \begin{vmatrix} u_i & v_i \\ u_i & v_i \end{vmatrix} = u_i v_i - u_i v_i = 0\]

3.1.3 Exterior Derivative of a Function (0‑Forms)

Definition 3.5 Let \(f : U \subseteq \mathbb{R}^n \to \mathbb{R}\) be a smooth function.
Its differential (also called the exterior derivative of a 0‑form) is the 1‑form

\[ df = \sum_{i=1}^n \frac{\partial f}{\partial x_i}\, dx_i. \]

In particular, for \(U \subseteq \mathbb{R}^3\), \[ df = f_x\,dx + f_y\,dy + f_z\,dz. \]

3.1.4 Differential Forms on Riemann Surfaces

Let \(M\) be a Riemann surface.

  • A \(0\)-form on \(M\) is a function on \(M\).
  • A \(1\)-form \(\omega\) on \(M\) is an assignment of two continuous functions \(f\) and \(g\) to each local coordinate \(z = x + iy\) on \(M\) such that \(\omega = f\,dx + g\,dy\).
  • A \(2\)-form \(\Omega\) on \(M\) is an assignment of a continuous function \(f\) to each local coordinate \(z\) such that \(f(z)\,dx \wedge dy\) is invariant under coordinate changes. That is, in terms of another local coordinate \(\tilde{z}\) we have:

\[\tilde{f}(\tilde{z}) = f(z(\tilde{z})) \frac{\partial(x,y)}{\partial(\tilde{x},\tilde{y})}\]

where \(\frac{\partial(x,y)}{\partial(\tilde{x},\tilde{y})}\) is the Jacobian determinant.

Definition 3.6 (Exterior Derivative of a $k$-form)) The exterior derivative of a \(k\)-form \(\omega = \sum f_{i_1 \dots i_k} dx_{i_1} \wedge \dots \wedge dx_{i_k}\) on \(U \subseteq \mathbb{R}^n\) is the \((k+1)\)-form \(d\omega\) given by:

\[d\omega = \sum_{1 \le i_1 < \dots < i_k \le n} d(f_{i_1 \dots i_k}) \wedge dx_{i_1} \wedge \dots \wedge dx_{i_k}\]

where \(d(f_{i_1 \dots i_k})\) means the exterior derivative of the function (\(0\)-form).

3.1.5 \(k\)-Forms in \(\mathbb{R}^3\)

Definition 3.7 (Forms in R3) Let \(U \subseteq \mathbb{R}^3\) be an open subset.

  1. A zero-form is a smooth function \(f: U \to \mathbb{R}\).
  2. A one-form is an expression \(f\,dx + g\,dy + h\,dz\) for smooth functions \(f,g,h: U \to \mathbb{R}\).
  3. A two-form is an expression \(f\,dy \wedge dz + g\,dz \wedge dx + h\,dx \wedge dy\) for smooth functions \(f,g,h: U \to \mathbb{R}\).
  4. A three-form is an expression \(f\,dx \wedge dy \wedge dz\) for a smooth function \(f: U \to \mathbb{R}\).

3.1.6 \(k\)-Forms and Vector Calculus

There is a natural correspondence between differential forms in \(\mathbb{R}^3\) and concepts from vector calculus:

Form Degree Differential Form Vector Calculus Concept
\(0\)-form Function \(f\) Scalar Field \(f\)
\(1\)-form \(f\,dx + g\,dy + h\,dz\) Vector Field \(\mathbf{F} = (f,g,h)\)
\(2\)-form \(f\,dy \wedge dz + g\,dz \wedge dx + h\,dx \wedge dy\) Vector Field \(\mathbf{F} = (f,g,h)\)
\(3\)-form \(f\,dx \wedge dy \wedge dz\) Scalar Field \(f\)

Example 3.1 Show that \(dz \wedge dx \wedge dy = dx \wedge dy \wedge dz.\)

Solution Using antisymmetry of the wedge product,

\[ dz \wedge dx = -\, dx \wedge dz, \qquad dz \wedge dy = -\, dy \wedge dz. \]

Thus,

\[ dz \wedge dx \wedge dy = -\, dx \wedge dz \wedge dy = dx \wedge dy \wedge dz. \]

Example 3.2 List the independent non-vanishing basic \(k\)-forms in \(\mathbb{R}^4\).

Solution: Because of antisymmetry, the only non-vanishing basic \(k\)-forms in \(\mathbb{R}^4\) occur for \(k \le 4\).

  • Basic one-forms:

\[ dx_1,\quad dx_2,\quad dx_3,\quad dx_4. \]

  • Basic two-forms:

\[ dx_1 \wedge dx_2,\quad dx_1 \wedge dx_3,\quad dx_1 \wedge dx_4,\quad dx_2 \wedge dx_3,\quad dx_2 \wedge dx_4,\quad dx_3 \wedge dx_4. \]

  • Basic three-forms:

\[ dx_1 \wedge dx_2 \wedge dx_3,\quad dx_1 \wedge dx_2 \wedge dx_4,\quad dx_1 \wedge dx_3 \wedge dx_4,\quad dx_2 \wedge dx_3 \wedge dx_4. \]

  • Basic four-form:

\[ dx_1 \wedge dx_2 \wedge dx_3 \wedge dx_4. \]

There is only one independent basic four-form, since repeated factors would make the wedge product vanish.

Example 3.3 Write down the vector field \(F : \mathbb{R}^3 \to \mathbb{R}^3\) associated to the two-form

\[ \omega = xy\, dx \wedge dy + xyz\, dx \wedge dz + x^2\, dy \wedge dz. \]

solution: A general two-form in \(\mathbb{R}^3\) can be written as

\[ \omega = f\, dy \wedge dz + g\, dz \wedge dx + h\, dx \wedge dy, \]

and it corresponds to the vector field \((f, g, h)\).

Rewrite \(\omega\) in this order:

\[ \omega = x^2\, dy \wedge dz - xyz\, dz \wedge dx + xy\, dx \wedge dy. \]

Thus the associated vector field is

\[ F(x,y,z) = (x^2,\; -xyz,\; xy). \]

Example 3.4 Let \(\omega = dx \wedge dz\) be a basic two-form on \(\mathbb{R}^3\), and let \(u = (1,2,3)\) and \(v = (3,2,1)\). Evaluate \(\omega(u,v)\).

solution: We compute

\[ \omega(u,v) = (dx \wedge dz)(u,v) = \begin{vmatrix} u_1 & v_1 \\ u_3 & v_3 \end{vmatrix} = \begin{vmatrix} 1 & 3 \\ 3 & 1 \end{vmatrix} = 1 - 9 = -8. \]

Example 3.5 Let \(\omega = dx \wedge dy \wedge dz\) be the basic three-form on \(\mathbb{R}^3\).

  1. Show that \(\omega(u,v,w)=1\) for \(u=(1,0,0)\), \(v=(0,1,0)\), \(w=(0,0,1)\).
  2. Show that \(\omega(v,u,w)=-1\).
  3. Show that exactly three orderings of \((u,v,w)\) give \(+1\) and three give \(-1\).

solution (a) We compute

\[ \omega(u,v,w) = \begin{vmatrix} u_1 & v_1 & w_1 \\ u_2 & v_2 & w_2 \\ u_3 & v_3 & w_3 \end{vmatrix} = \begin{vmatrix} 1 & 0 & 0 \\ 0 & 1 & 0 \\ 0 & 0 & 1 \end{vmatrix} = 1. \]

  1. Swapping \(u\) and \(v\) exchanges two columns of the determinant:

\[ \omega(v,u,w) = \begin{vmatrix} v_1 & u_1 & w_1 \\ v_2 & u_2 & w_2 \\ v_3 & u_3 & w_3 \end{vmatrix} = - \begin{vmatrix} u_1 & v_1 & w_1 \\ u_2 & v_2 & w_2 \\ u_3 & v_3 & w_3 \end{vmatrix} = -1. \]

  1. Any permutation of \((u,v,w)\) corresponds to permuting the columns of the determinant.
    An even number of column swaps preserves the sign; an odd number reverses it.

Thus the following three orderings give \(+1\):

\[ (u,v,w),\quad (w,u,v),\quad (v,w,u), \]

i.e.  \[ dx \wedge dy \wedge dz(\mathbf{u},\mathbf{v},\mathbf{w}) = dx \wedge dy \wedge dz(\mathbf{w},\mathbf{u},\mathbf{v}) = dx \wedge dy \wedge dz(\mathbf{v},\mathbf{w},\mathbf{u}) = 1, \] and the following three give \(-1\):

\[ (v,u,w),\quad (w,v,u),\quad (u,w,v). \]

i.e.

\[ dx \wedge dy \wedge dz(\mathbf{v},\mathbf{u},\mathbf{w}) = dx \wedge dy \wedge dz(\mathbf{w},\mathbf{v},\mathbf{u}) = dx \wedge dy \wedge dz(\mathbf{u},\mathbf{w},\mathbf{v}) = -1. \]

The reason is that permuting two basis vectors corresponds to swapping two columns of the matrix whose determinant defines the value of the \(3\)‑form. Since exchanging two columns changes the determinant by a factor of \(-1\), any even number of such swaps leaves the determinant unchanged, whereas any odd number of swaps multiplies it by \(-1\).

3.2 Wedge Product

Definition 3.8 (Wedge Product) Consider a simple \(k\)-form \(\omega\) and a simple \(m\)-form \(\eta\) on an open subset \(U \subseteq \mathbb{R}^n\) of the form

\[ \omega = f\, dx_{i_1} \wedge \cdots \wedge dx_{i_k}, \qquad \eta = g\, dx_{j_1} \wedge \cdots \wedge dx_{j_m}, \]

for smooth functions \(f, g : U \to \mathbb{R}\).

The \(\omega \wedge \eta\) is the \((k+m)\)-form defined by

\[ \omega \wedge \eta = fg\, dx_{i_1} \wedge \cdots \wedge dx_{i_k} \wedge dx_{j_1} \wedge \cdots \wedge dx_{j_m}. \]

Note that the order of the \(dx_i\)’s is important, since exchanging two differentials introduces a sign by antisymmetry.

The wedge product of arbitrary \(k\)- and \(m\)-forms is defined by linearity, i.e. by distributing the wedge product term-by-term over sums.

Definition 3.9 (Wedge Product) The wedge product \(\wedge\) combines a \(k\)-form and an \(m\)-form to yield a \((k+m)\)-form. It is associative, bilinear, and satisfies graded commutativity:

\[\omega \wedge \eta = (-1)^{km} \eta \wedge \omega\]

3.2.1 Examples of Wedge Product

Example 3.6 (Wedge product of two 1-forms) Let \(\omega = x\,dx + x\,dy + e^z\,dz\) and \(\eta = y\,dx + x\,dy + z\,dz\). Then:

\[\begin{aligned} \omega \wedge \eta &= (x\,dx + x\,dy + e^z\,dz) \wedge (y\,dx + x\,dy + z\,dz) \\ &= (x^2 - xy)\,dx \wedge dy + (xz + e^z y)\,dz \wedge dx + (xz - e^z x)\,dz \wedge dy \end{aligned}\]

Example 3.7 (Wedge product of a 1-form and a 2-form) Let \(\omega = xy\,dx + y\,dy\) and \(\eta = z\,dy \wedge dz\). Then:

\[\omega \wedge \eta = (xy\,dx + y\,dy) \wedge (z\,dy \wedge dz) = xyz\,dx \wedge dy \wedge dz\]

Example 3.8 Let \(\omega = x\,dy \wedge dz + y\,dz \wedge dx + z\,dx \wedge dy\) and \(\eta = x\,dx + y\,dy + z\,dz.\)

Solution: We compute the wedge product, noting that any term containing a repeated \(dx, dy,\) or \(dz\) vanishes: \[\begin{align*} \omega \wedge \eta &= (x\,dy \wedge dz + y\,dz \wedge dx + z\,dx \wedge dy) \wedge (x\,dx + y\,dy + z\,dz) \\[4pt] &= x^{2}\,dy \wedge dz \wedge dx + y^{2}\,dz \wedge dx \wedge dy + z^{2}\,dx \wedge dy \wedge dz. \end{align*}\]

Since

\[ dy \wedge dz \wedge dx = dz \wedge dx \wedge dy = dx \wedge dy \wedge dz, \]

we obtain the standard form:

\[ \omega \wedge \eta = (x^{2} + y^{2} + z^{2})\,dx \wedge dy \wedge dz. \]

Example 3.10 Let \(\omega = f\,dx + g\,dy + h\,dz\) be an arbitrary one-form on \(\mathbb{R}^3.\) Show that \(\omega \wedge \omega = 0.\)

Solution: We compute directly, \[\begin{align*} \omega \wedge \omega &= (f\,dx + g\,dy + h\,dz) \wedge (f\,dx + g\,dy + h\,dz) \\[4pt] &= fg\,dx \wedge dy + fh\,dx \wedge dz + gf\,dy \wedge dx + gh\,dy \wedge dz + hf\,dz \wedge dx + hg\,dz \wedge dy. \end{align*}\]

Grouping terms and using the antisymmetry of the wedge product,

\[ dy \wedge dx = -dx \wedge dy, \quad dz \wedge dx = -dx \wedge dz, \quad dz \wedge dy = -dy \wedge dz, \]

we obtain

\[ \omega \wedge \omega = (gh - hg)\,dy \wedge dz + (hf - fh)\,dz \wedge dx + (fg - gf)\,dx \wedge dy. \]

Since scalar functions commute, i.e. \(gh = hg, \ hf = fh, \ fg = gf\), it follows that

\[ \omega \wedge \omega = 0 \]

Example 3.11 Find the cross-product of the vectors \(\mathbf{F} = (1,1,0)\) and \(\mathbf{G} = (0,2,3)\) in \(\mathbb{R}^3\) by computing the wedge product of the associated one-forms.

Solution : The one-forms associated to \(\mathbf{F}\) and \(\mathbf{G}\) are

\[ \omega = dx + dy, \qquad \eta = 2\,dy + 3\,dz. \]

Compute the wedge product: \[\begin{align*} \omega \wedge \eta &= (dx + dy) \wedge (2\,dy + 3\,dz) \\[4pt] &= 2\,dx \wedge dy + 3\,dx \wedge dz + 3\,dy \wedge dz. \end{align*}\]

Rewriting in standard orientation,

\[ \omega \wedge \eta = 3\,dy \wedge dz - 3\,dz \wedge dx + 2\,dx \wedge dy. \]

By the dictionary between two-forms and vector fields,

\[ \mathbf{F} \times \mathbf{G} = (3,\,-3,\,2). \]

Thus the wedge product correctly reproduces the cross-product.

Example 3.12 Let \(\mathbf{A},\, \mathbf{B},\, \mathbf{C} \in\mathbb{R}^3.\) Show that \[\mathbf{A}\cdot(\mathbf{B}\times\mathbf{C}) =\mathbf{B}\cdot(\mathbf{C}\times\mathbf{A}) =\mathbf{C}\cdot(\mathbf{A}\times\mathbf{B})\] using wedge products of the associated one-forms.

Solution: Let \(\omega, \eta, \lambda\) be the one-forms associated to \(\mathbf{A}, \mathbf{B}, \mathbf{C}\).
From the dictionary for one-forms and vector fields,

\[ \omega \wedge \eta \wedge \lambda = (\mathbf{A}\cdot(\mathbf{B}\times\mathbf{C}))\, dx\wedge dy\wedge dz, \]

\[ \eta \wedge \lambda \wedge \omega = (\mathbf{B}\cdot(\mathbf{C}\times\mathbf{A}))\, dx\wedge dy\wedge dz, \]

\[ \lambda \wedge \omega \wedge \eta = (\mathbf{C}\cdot(\mathbf{A}\times\mathbf{B}))\, dx\wedge dy\wedge dz. \]

Since all three are one-forms, wedge products anticommute:

\[ \omega \wedge \eta = -\,\eta \wedge \omega, \qquad \eta \wedge \lambda = -\,\lambda \wedge \eta, \qquad \omega \wedge \lambda = -\,\lambda \wedge \omega. \]

Thus,

\[ \omega \wedge \eta \wedge \lambda = \eta \wedge \lambda \wedge \omega = \lambda \wedge \omega \wedge \eta. \]

Therefore,

\[ \mathbf{A}\cdot(\mathbf{B}\times\mathbf{C}) = \mathbf{B}\cdot(\mathbf{C}\times\mathbf{A}) = \mathbf{C}\cdot(\mathbf{A}\times\mathbf{B}), \]

which follows directly from the antisymmetry of the wedge product.

3.3 Exterior Derivative

Definition 3.10 (Exterior Derivative of a k-Form) The exterior derivative of a zero‑form \(f\) on \(U \subset \mathbb{R}^n\) is the one‑form
\[ df = \sum_{i=1}^{n} \frac{\partial f}{\partial x_i}\, dx_i, \] which coincides with the differential introduced earlier.

The exterior derivative of a \(k\)-form
\[ \omega = \sum_{1 \le i_1 < \cdots < i_k \le n} f_{i_1 \cdots i_k}\, dx_{i_1} \wedge \cdots \wedge dx_{i_k} \] on \(U \subset \mathbb{R}^n\) is the \((k+1)\)-form
\[ d\omega = \sum_{1 \le i_1 < \cdots < i_k \le n} d(f_{i_1 \cdots i_k}) \wedge dx_{i_1} \wedge \cdots \wedge dx_{i_k}, \] where \(d(f_{i_1 \cdots i_k})\) is the exterior derivative of the scalar function \(f_{i_1 \cdots i_k}\).
In other words, the operator \(d\) is applied to the component functions of \(\omega\).

Lemma 3.5 (Exterior Derivative in R^3)

  1. Zero‑forms: If \(f\) is a zero‑form on \(U \subset \mathbb{R}^3\), then

\[df= \frac{\partial f}{\partial x}\, dx + \frac{\partial f}{\partial y}\, dy + \frac{\partial f}{\partial z}\, dz.\]

  1. One‑forms If \(\omega = f\, dx + g\, dy + h\, dz,\) then

\[\begin{align} d\omega &= d(f) \wedge dx + d(g) \wedge dy + d(h) \wedge dz \\[4pt] &= \left( \frac{\partial h}{\partial y} - \frac{\partial g}{\partial z} \right) dy \wedge dz + \left( \frac{\partial f}{\partial z} - \frac{\partial h}{\partial x} \right) dz \wedge dx + \left( \frac{\partial g}{\partial x} - \frac{\partial f}{\partial y} \right) dx \wedge dy. \end{align}\]

  1. Two‑forms: If \(\eta = f\, dy \wedge dz + g\, dz \wedge dx + h\, dx \wedge dy,\) then

\[\begin{align} d\eta &= d(f) \wedge dy \wedge dz+ d(g) \wedge dz \wedge dx+ d(h) \wedge dx \wedge dy,\\ &= \left(\frac{\partial f}{\partial x}+ \frac{\partial g}{\partial y}+ \frac{\partial h}{\partial z}\right)dx \wedge dy \wedge dz. \end{align}\]

Finally, the exterior derivative of any three‑form in \(\mathbb{R}^3\) must vanish, since it would produce a four‑form, and all \(k\)-forms with \(k \ge 4\) are identically zero in \(\mathbb{R}^3\).

Proof.

General Case

Write \(\omega\) and \(\eta\) in local coordinates as \[ \omega = \sum_{I} f_I\, dx_{i_1} \wedge \cdots \wedge dx_{i_k}, \qquad \eta = \sum_{J} g_J\, dx_{j_1} \wedge \cdots \wedge dx_{j_l}, \] where \(I = (i_1 < \cdots < i_k)\), \(J = (j_1 < \cdots < j_l)\), and \(f_I, g_J\) are smooth functions.

Their wedge product is \[ \omega \wedge \eta = \sum_{I,J} f_I g_J\, dx_{i_1} \wedge \cdots \wedge dx_{i_k} \wedge dx_{j_1} \wedge \cdots \wedge dx_{j_l}. \]

Applying the exterior derivative and using linearity, \[ d(\omega \wedge \eta) = \sum_{I,J} d(f_I g_J) \wedge dx_{i_1} \wedge \cdots \wedge dx_{i_k} \wedge dx_{j_1} \wedge \cdots \wedge dx_{j_l}. \]

By the product rule for functions (the 0‑form case you already proved), \[ d(f_I g_J) = df_I\, g_J + f_I\, dg_J. \] Hence \[ d(\omega \wedge \eta) = \sum_{I,J} df_I\, g_J \wedge dx_I \wedge dx_J + \sum_{I,J} f_I\, dg_J \wedge dx_I \wedge dx_J, \] where \(dx_I = dx_{i_1} \wedge \cdots \wedge dx_{i_k}\) and \(dx_J = dx_{j_1} \wedge \cdots \wedge dx_{j_l}\).

For the first sum, \[ \sum_{I,J} df_I\, g_J \wedge dx_I \wedge dx_J = \left(\sum_I df_I \wedge dx_I\right) \wedge \left(\sum_J g_J dx_J\right) = d\omega \wedge \eta. \]

For the second sum, note that \(dg_J\) is a 1‑form and \(dx_I\) is a \(k\)-form, so \[ dg_J \wedge dx_I = (-1)^k\, dx_I \wedge dg_J. \] Thus \[ \sum_{I,J} f_I\, dg_J \wedge dx_I \wedge dx_J = (-1)^k \sum_{I,J} f_I\, dx_I \wedge dg_J \wedge dx_J = (-1)^k\, \omega \wedge d\eta. \]

Combining both parts, \[ d(\omega \wedge \eta) = d\omega \wedge \eta + (-1)^k\, \omega \wedge d\eta, \] which proves the lemma.

Remark. Graded product rule in \(\mathbb{R}^3\)

In \(\mathbb{R}^3\), the graded product rule splits into the following non-vanishing cases.

  • If \(\omega = f\) is a zero-form and \(\eta = g\) is a zero-form, then

\[ d(fg) = d(f)\,g + f\,d(g). \]

  • If \(\omega = f\) is a zero-form and \(\eta\) is a one-form, then

\[ d(f\eta) = d(f) \wedge \eta + f\, d(\eta). \]

  • If \(\omega = f\) is a zero-form and \(\eta\) is a two-form, then

\[ d(f\eta) = d(f) \wedge \eta + f\, d(\eta). \]

  • If \(\omega\) is a one-form and \(\eta\) is a one-form, then

\[ d(\omega \wedge \eta) = d(\omega) \wedge \eta - \omega \wedge d(\eta). \]

3.3.2 Grad, Curl, and Div Connection

Definition (Gradient): For a \(0\)-form \(f\):

\[df = \frac{\partial f}{\partial x}dx + \frac{\partial f}{\partial y}dy + \frac{\partial f}{\partial z}dz \quad \longleftrightarrow \quad \nabla f = \left(\frac{\partial f}{\partial x}, \frac{\partial f}{\partial y}, \frac{\partial f}{\partial z}\right)\]

Definition (Curl): For a \(1\)-form \(\omega = f_1\,dx + f_2\,dy + f_3\,dz\), the \(2\)-form \(d\omega\) corresponds to \(\nabla \times \mathbf{F}\):

\[d\omega = \left(\frac{\partial f_3}{\partial y} - \frac{\partial f_2}{\partial z}\right)dy \wedge dz + \left(\frac{\partial f_1}{\partial z} - \frac{\partial f_3}{\partial x}\right)dz \wedge dx + \left(\frac{\partial f_2}{\partial x} - \frac{\partial f_1}{\partial y}\right)dx \wedge dy\]

Definition (Divergence): For a \(2\)-form \(\eta = f_1\,dy \wedge dz + f_2\,dz \wedge dx + f_3\,dx \wedge dy\), the \(3\)-form \(d\eta\) corresponds to \(\nabla \cdot \mathbf{F}\):

\[d\eta = \left(\frac{\partial f_1}{\partial x} + \frac{\partial f_2}{\partial y} + \frac{\partial f_3}{\partial z}\right)dx \wedge dy \wedge dz\]

3.4 The Hodge Star Operator

Definition (Hodge Star Dual): Let \(\omega = dx_{i_1} \wedge \dots \wedge dx_{i_k}\) be a basic \(k\)-form on \(\mathbb{R}^n\). The Hodge star dual \(*\omega\) is the unique basic \((n-k)\)-form satisfying:

\[\omega \wedge *\omega = dx_1 \wedge \dots \wedge dx_n\]

Extended linearly, for a general \(k\)-form \(\eta = \sum f_{i_1 \dots i_k} dx_{i_1} \wedge \dots \wedge dx_{i_k}\):

\[*\eta = \sum f_{i_1 \dots i_k} *(dx_{i_1} \wedge \dots \wedge dx_{i_k})\]

3.4.1 Hodge Star in \(\mathbb{R}^3\)

In \(\mathbb{R}^3\) with standard Euclidean metric:

\[\begin{aligned} *1 &= dx \wedge dy \wedge dz \\ *dx &= dy \wedge dz, \quad *dy = dz \wedge dx, \quad *dz = dx \wedge dy \\ *(dy \wedge dz) &= dx, \quad *(dz \wedge dx) = dy, \quad *(dx \wedge dy) = dz \\ *(dx \wedge dy \wedge dz) &= 1 \end{aligned}\]

Proposition: On \(k\)-forms in \(\mathbb{R}^n\), applying the Hodge star twice yields:

\[**\omega = (-1)^{k(n-k)} \omega\]

3.5 Integration, Chains, and Stokes’ Theorem

3.5.1 Simplices and Chains

  • \(0\)-simplex: Point \(P\).
  • \(1\)-simplex: Directed segment \(\langle P_1, P_2 \rangle\).
  • \(2\)-simplex: Oriented triangle \(\langle P_1, P_2, P_3 \rangle\).

An \(n\)-chain \(c\) is a formal integer linear combination of \(n\)-simplices:

\[c = \sum_i m_i \sigma_i, \quad m_i \in \mathbb{Z}\]

3.5.2 Stokes’ Theorem

Theorem (Generalized Stokes’ Theorem):
If \(\omega\) is a \(C^1\) \(k\)-form and \(D\) is a \((k+1)\)-chain, then

\[ \int_{\partial D} \omega = \int_D d\omega. \]

3.6 Complex Operators and Harmonic Forms

On a Riemann surface with complex coordinate \(z = x + iy\):

\[\begin{aligned} \partial f &= f_z\,dz = \frac{1}{2}(f_x - i f_y)\,dz \\ \bar{\partial} f &= f_{\bar{z}}\,d\bar{z} = \frac{1}{2}(f_x + i f_y)\,d\bar{z} \end{aligned}\]

The exterior derivative decomposes as \(d = \partial + \bar{\partial}\).

Definition (Laplacian and Harmonic Forms): The Laplacian of a function \(f\) is:

\[\Delta f = (f_{xx} + f_{yy})\,dx \wedge dy = -2i\,\partial \bar{\partial} f\]

A \(1\)-form \(\omega\) is harmonic if it is locally given by \(df\) where \(\Delta f = 0\).

Proposition: A differential form \(\omega\) is harmonic if and only if it is both closed (\(d\omega = 0\)) and co-closed (\(d*\omega = 0\)).
